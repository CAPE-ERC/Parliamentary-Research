CAPE — Mauritius Parliamentary Discourse Research

Procedural Power & Policy Attention Pipeline

A four-layer computational pipeline over 411 Hansard debates of the Mauritius National Assembly (2015–2025), testing whether control over parliamentary procedure shapes which policy topics receive sustained attention. This page tracks implementation progress, methodology, and results layer by layer.

All five layers of the pipeline — Layer 0 (preprocessing), the Topic layer (two successive classifier generations, evaluated comparatively), the Procedural layer (rule-based tagging combined with an LSTM sequence classifier), the Linking layer (mixed-effects regression), and the Outcome layer (a Granger-style time-series analysis) — are complete and merged to main. All three hypotheses under investigation returned genuine null results once adequately powered and formally tested, each requiring substantial preparatory work before testing could proceed. H1 (asymmetric procedural enforcement) required construction of a verified speaker/party registry, absent which the government-versus-opposition comparison would have relied on a severely underpowered proxy. H3 (attention cooling following procedural conflict) required a complete, chronologically ordered and zero-filled time series rather than an index restricted to a topic's subsequent appearances. H2 (question deflection) required unbundling a routine Speaker announcement into individually identified Parliamentary Questions and resolving each question's asker, which recovered 12,426 testable questions from an initially sparse 226-utterance signal.

Each debate transcript is parsed with pdfplumber and segmented into speaker-attributed utterances using a regular-expression tagging scheme derived from Hansard's own formatting conventions, including multi-line "long-form" speaker tags such as "The Vice-Prime Minister, Minister of Energy and Public Utilities (Mr I. Collendavelloo):". Role and party are resolved against each debate's own printed Cabinet and Principal Officers list, so that ministerial reshuffles are captured automatically at the sitting level rather than assumed static across the corpus. Stage directions (interruptions, sitting suspensions) are retained as a distinct structural signal rather than merged into speech content.

Substitution of langdetect for fastText. The original specification called for fastText-based language identification; fastText requires a C++ build toolchain that was unavailable in the development environment. langdetect, supplemented by a Kreol Morisien keyword heuristic, was substituted instead. This deviation is documented in the codebase rather than applied without record.
Identification and correction of a role-resolution gap. Generic role titles — "The Deputy Speaker," "The Deputy Prime Minister," "The Chairperson" — were not matched by the original role patterns and were consequently classified as unclassified_mp. This was corrected in src/preprocessing/roles.py, and the corpus was reprocessed accordingly: 20,064 utterances were reassigned from unclassified_mp to their correct chair or government role.

Model v2 decomposes classification into a binary Policy/non_policy gate followed by a 50-way domain classifier trained exclusively on Policy examples, testing the hypothesis that the majority of v1's error originated from the non_policy catch-all category rather than from difficulty distinguishing among the 50 domains themselves. Linking Layer

Intervention rate ~ topic × speaker party × chair (MixedLM)

Rationale for constructing a verified speaker/party registry prior to regression. Preliminary examination of the data found that only 145 of 211,567 utterances had a resolved party = opposition value, all attributable to a single office (Leader of the Opposition), compared with 39,396 utterances resolved as government. Testing H1 on this basis would, in effect, have compared the Leader of the Opposition against Ministers rather than estimating a party-level effect. A verified speaker/party registry (approximately 198 Members of Parliament across three Assembly terms, incorporating a time-aware correction for PMSD's departure from government in December 2016) was constructed to resolve backbench Members instead, extending the usable panel from 145 opposition utterances to 13,032.

H1 — Asymmetric Enforcement: No Significant Effect

The model specification intervention_rate ~ C(topic) × C(party), with a random intercept by presiding Chair, was fit on the properly powered panel. Only 1 of 47 topic × opposition interaction terms is significant at p < 0.05, consistent with the rate expected by chance across 47 independent tests (approximately 2.35 false positives expected under the null hypothesis), and this term does not survive Bonferroni correction. Moreover, the one nominally significant term (Vaccination/COVID-19, p = 0.044) points in the direction opposite to that predicted by H1, and this topic does not fall within the corruption, procurement, or institutional-scandal category around which H1 was originally framed.

H2 — Question Deflection: No Significant Effect

The pnq_transfer tag is genuinely rare at the utterance level, occurring in 226 of 211,567 utterances corpus-wide, and an initial per-topic breakdown left most cells with zero to two events — insufficient signal to draw a conclusion. Examination of a sample of these utterances found the source of the sparsity: 84% are the Speaker's routine end-of-Question-Time announcement, each bundling a median of three unanswered questions together, a scheduling artifact rather than 226 independent decisions. Every Parliamentary Question additionally carries a (No. B/xxx) <Name> (<Constituency>) header in the same convention already parsed for roles in Layer 0, permitting each question's asker to be identified directly.

Rebuilding H2 at individual-question granularity. Unbundling the transfer announcements and resolving each question's asker via the existing speaker registry (src/linking_layer/pq_deflection.py) recovers 12,426 distinct Parliamentary Questions corpus-wide, of which 1,041 were transferred and 11,240 have a resolved asker party — sufficient to test H2 as originally framed: whether a question's fate depends on who asked it.

Outcome Layer

Topic attention share in t+1 ~ procedural conflict in t (Granger-style + MixedLM)

This layer tests H3, the feedback-loop hypothesis within the project's conceptual framework: whether procedural conflict on a given topic in one sitting depresses that topic's subsequent attention share. A complete topic × sitting grid was constructed — 50 policy topics across all 402 chronologically ordered sittings, with 2 of 404 sittings excluded owing to unparseable dates — and zero-filled for sittings in which a topic was not raised, so that no series advances directly to a topic's next appearance. Procedural conflict is operationalized using the same windowed chair_ruling/interruption definition applied in H1 and H2, aggregated to a per-topic, per-sitting rate.

H3 — Attention Cooling: No Significant Effect

Per-topic Granger causality tests (lag 1, conflict_rate → attention_share) were conducted for the 38 topics exhibiting sufficient conflict-sitting variation to permit meaningful testing. 3 of 38 topics are significant at raw p < 0.05 — a rate consistent with the approximately 1.9 false positives expected by chance — and only 1 survives Bonferroni correction (α = 0.05/38). A pooled MixedLM panel model (attention_sharet+1 ~ attention_sharet + conflict_ratet, with a random intercept by topic) corroborates this result: the coefficient on conflict_rate is effectively zero (−0.0002, p = 0.897), whereas attention_sharet itself is the only substantive predictor of attention_sharet+1 (coefficient 0.177, p < 0.001). Topics that receive substantial attention continue to do so; there is no measurable evidence that procedural conflict reduces subsequent attention.

This result constitutes a genuine null finding rather than a power artifact: the full 402-sitting series was available for every topic, and the two independent tests (per-topic Granger causality and the pooled panel model) are in agreement. Considered alongside the null result for H1, the corpus provides no broad evidence that procedural friction is employed to selectively suppress particular topics, either within individual sittings (H1) or across time (H3).

Caveats

  • Granger causality presupposes stationary series. A 2015–2025 span may plausibly contain trends or structural breaks (for example, COVID-era spikes in health-related topics) that were not assessed through per-topic ADF unit-root testing, given the scope of this analysis; results should accordingly be regarded as suggestive rather than confirmatory
  • 12 of 50 topics exhibited insufficient conflict-sitting variation (fewer than 10 sittings with conflict_rate > 0) to permit meaningful testing and were excluded; this exclusion does not constitute evidence of an absent effect for those topics specifically
  • The pooled MixedLM's full model summary (beyond the coefficients quoted above) is in outcome_layer_report.md

Interpretation of Findings

A non-technical summary of the results

A functioning parliament depends on ensuring that no single actor, whether the governing party or the opposition, determines unilaterally which issues receive public attention. This guarantee is not self-enforcing. Chair rulings, points of order, interruptions, and the deferral of questions to later sittings are ordinary and necessary instruments for the conduct of orderly debate. In principle, however, the same instruments could be applied unevenly: more frequently against whichever party does not hold power, or more frequently in relation to subjects a government would prefer not to address. Whether such asymmetry is present within the Mauritius National Assembly cannot be established through informal observation of a limited number of sittings; it requires systematic analysis of the complete debate record.

This analysis draws on that complete record: 411 debates spanning 2015 to 2025, comprising more than 211,000 individual utterances. Three specific, falsifiable hypotheses were tested, rather than a single general claim. Establishing a valid basis for comparison required substantial preparatory work. An initial attempt to identify opposition speakers yielded only 145 utterances, all attributable to a single office (Leader of the Opposition); a comparison of government and opposition speech on this basis would not have been meaningful. Correctly identifying the party affiliation of every backbench Member of Parliament — drawing on the results of three general elections and accounting for a mid-term coalition realignment — extended the usable sample to 13,032 opposition utterances prior to the commencement of hypothesis testing.

Summary of hypotheses and findings

HypothesisFormulationFinding
H1 — Asymmetric enforcement Whether the Chair intervenes — through rulings or interruptions — more frequently when the opposition raises a given topic than when the government does. No significant effect Of 47 topic-by-party interaction terms tested, only one differed from chance expectation, and its direction was opposite to that predicted by the asymmetric-enforcement hypothesis.
H2 — Question deflection Whether questions on sensitive subjects are deferred to a later sitting more frequently than questions on routine subjects. No significant effect Of 11,240 individually identified questions with a known asker, the transfer rate for opposition-asked questions (8.01%) does not differ significantly from that for government-asked questions (7.77%), including when restricted to sensitive policy subjects specifically.
H3 — Attention cooling Whether a procedurally contentious exchange on a given topic reduces subsequent attention to that topic. No significant effect Subsequent attention to a topic is predicted primarily by its prior level of attention rather than by the degree of procedural conflict it generated. Observed fluctuations correspond more closely to external events, such as a public-health emergency or the completion of an infrastructure project, than to procedural friction.

Three null findings might, on a superficial reading, be interpreted as an absence of results. Such an interpretation would understate their significance. The analytic pipeline was designed specifically to detect a subtle and structural form of bias, incorporating statistical safeguards — including Bonferroni correction applied across each set of per-topic tests — to prevent isolated, chance-level findings from being mistaken for a systematic pattern, and, in the case of H2, unbundling a sparse utterance-level signal into an individually identified, adequately powered population before concluding that no effect was present. Having applied this design, the analysis finds no broad evidence that procedural authority is being exercised to selectively suppress particular voices or subjects, whether within individual sittings, across time, or in the handling of individual questions. This constitutes a substantive empirical finding regarding institutional function along the three dimensions examined.

These findings should not be interpreted as a comprehensive assessment of procedural fairness within the institution. The analysis is limited to a single legislature over an eleven-year period and should not be generalized beyond that context without further study, and each test examines a specific, narrowly defined mechanism rather than procedural power in general. The validity of a null result depends directly on the quality of the underlying data; for this reason, construction of the speaker registry, correction of role-resolution errors, standardization of chair identification, and resolution of individual question askers were each completed prior to hypothesis testing, rather than in response to preliminary results.

Summary of findings. The analysis finds no broad evidence of asymmetric procedural enforcement (H1), targeted deflection of questions by the asker's party (H2), or attention suppression following procedural conflict (H3), across three independently constructed and adequately powered tests.
